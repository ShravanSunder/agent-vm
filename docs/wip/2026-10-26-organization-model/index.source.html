Organization model

This page is the model we are agreeing before any design spec is written. Read the two short lists first: what you have decided, and your picks from the last review. Everything below them is detail and examples. Your decisions are marked as yours; anything I inferred is marked Proposed.

Decided so far

  • Organization first. People and bots are added to an organization, then join its workspaces as members or collaborators. (Details)
  • One Fleet central, several organizations, nothing shared or sent between them. A person may be in several organizations; a bot or a Router belongs to exactly one.
  • People never hold tool tokens. People members get work done by talking to a bot that has access, through the bot's own channel. (Example 11)
  • Only you log Routers in for now, for machines you set up; VMs are enrolled by a VM creator or platform you approved. Managers come later, per workspace. Once a Router is logged in, anyone at it can start sessions; you control that per Router. (Details)
  • One login model for every machine. Every machine will log in with short-lived tokens from an issuer you register: a device code you approve for machines you set up; your VM creator or a platform's OIDC for VMs, approved one by one or pre-approved. (Details)
  • Three steps to get there: build the new login; switch to it behind Cloudflare Access and re-enroll every machine; remove Access in the next change.
  • Routers run on Linux and Firecracker VMs, so everything (including the secret store) must work there, not only on a Mac.

What belongs to what

Your words: "you add people bot to organization. then join workspaces or allowed to as members, or collaborators"; "person can belong to any orgs but not agents"; "a single router only belongs to one organization"; "i dont want cross organization any sharing stuff or comm".

One example fleet; the names are made up. Organizations contain workspaces, and workspaces contain projects. The member, collaborator and allowed-machine rows inside a workspace point back at the people, bots and Routers added to the organization above them; they are references, not copies.

What the example shows:

  • You are in two organizations (Acme and Personal). People may be; bots and Fleet machines may not.
  • Hermes and Review bot are each a member of one workspace and a collaborator of the other.
  • Each Router belongs to one organization. Your Personal work runs on a different Router (Home VM Router), never on an Acme one.
  • A session is never added by hand. It joins the workspace of the project it was started for, on a Fleet machine allowed to serve that workspace.

Messages reach a workspace's members by two separate routes, both inside one organization:

  • Collaboration. A bot collaborator of a workspace and that workspace's members may message each other, both ways, while the collaboration exists. No workspace pair is needed.
  • Workspace pair. Members of workspace A may message members of workspace B only when the admin has allowed A → B. A pair has one direction: "Product may message Docs" does not let Docs message Product.

There is no collaboration or pair across organizations. People as collaborators are new in your model (the identity design only had bot collaborators); they would use the collaboration route. Proposed.

What this means for the Tool Portal

The Tool Portal's unit stays the workspace: one MCP endpoint per workspace, with that workspace's personas, tool profiles and approvals. What moves is who decides membership: Fleet central decides, and the Tool Portal checks the token it is handed against its own local state. The Tool Portal never calls Fleet central during a tool call.

How example 6 runs, as the identity design draws it:

A bot asks the sign-in service for its token itself, with its own credential; the sign-in service asks Fleet central whether the bot is a member of that workspace before it signs anything.

The identity design already registers each bot in one organization and keeps an allowlist of people per organization. What your model adds:

  • The organization comes first for every workspace role. Fleet central gives no membership, collaboration or token to anyone not in that workspace's organization.
  • Proposed consequence: leaving the organization ends its workspace roles (example 10; you have not decided this). For a bot, Fleet central sends removal notices to each workspace it was a member of, using the removal notices the identity design already plans for a removed bot (designed, not built).
  • People as collaborators. No change in the Tool Portal, because collaborators never use tools. Fleet central needs a way for a signed-in person to send collaboration messages, which the identity design only has for bots.

How a Router's identity and enrollment are kept

Your decisions: "we can add managers later but it can be a concept, right now its just owner"; people members "use existing channels or bots to communicate"; "a bot is an existing channel. talk to bots who have access"; for VMs, "it needs to be preauthorized ... something that can spin up vms and enroll them so a service account too".

  • Who logs a Router into an organization: you, the owner, for machines you set up. For VMs, a VM creator or platform you registered enrolls each VM it starts, within the limits you set; you approve the creator, not each VM, unless you set it to approve each. Today's login is the fleet operator token (you hold both roles).
  • Who starts sessions: anyone or anything that can operate a logged-in Router, for the workspaces that Router may serve. The system knows which Router and which session, not which human. You control starting by where Routers run (keeping them on machines only you use is your operating practice, not something the system proves), what each Router may serve, and revoking a Router when needed.
  • How people members get work done: they talk to a bot that has access, through the bot's own channel. The bot acts with its own authority and persona, recorded as the bot, plus "on behalf of" the person where the channel can identify them; carrying the requester through is designed later (Fleet framework F-30, F-35).
  • Managers: a concept for later; they are per workspace (F-29). What they may do is not decided; granting collaborators for their own workspace is a proposed later use.

The thing enrolled is one Router installation, not a computer: nothing is derived from hardware (Fleet framework F-34). The installation keeps a stable identity in its own saved state; the enrollment is Fleet central's record that this installation may connect.

What this means: replacing the Router program keeps the same identity and enrollment, because both live in the Router's saved state. In normal use only a fresh state needs a new enrollment, and then the old one should be revoked. The one exception is the switch to the new login (step 2), which re-enrolls every machine while keeping its saved identity. Anyone who can use that Router's unlocked secret store can act as that Router, so revoking the enrollment is the way to cut off a lost or copied machine. If members may later log Routers in, the enrollment would start as pending until you approve it, and only then connect (proposed, for later).

How machines log in: where this is going

Today every Router passes two checks, each with a long-lived secret: a Cloudflare Access service token (Cloudflare's gate in front of Fleet central, checked at Cloudflare's edge) and its enrollment credential (Fleet central's check of which Router it is). Long-lived means weeks or months: a leaked one works until it is revoked or expires.

Your decisions: "i like oidc idea ... can i use this for all my machines mac, linux vms everything? then when a new machine is created, i approve, or its pre approved depending on the type of identity system"; "we keep cloudflare access and build the other too, then we keep cloudflare access on until we ready to switch over"; "no half steps. after we switch over we remove cloudflare access"; then, on the steps: "everything in access guard till i can prove login works behind it"; "its accept behind no? not either?"; "yes, this is safer".

  • Every machine (Mac, Linux server, Firecracker or cloud VM) connects with a short-lived signed token from an issuer you registered. Fleet central checks the issuer, the machine and its one organization.
  • Approval depends on the issuer: machines you set up are approved by you one by one; an issuer you trust can be pre-approved within limits you set.
  • Three steps, no half steps:
    1. Build the new login completely. Machines keep today's login (Cloudflare Access plus the enrollment credential).
    2. Switch, behind Access. One change: Fleet central accepts only the new login, and the old enrollment credential stops working. Cloudflare Access stays in front of everything. You re-enroll every machine. If it breaks, machines are offline until it is fixed or the change is reverted, but nothing is exposed.
    3. Remove Access, in the next change, once step 2 works on every machine.

  • Device-code risk: someone starts a login on their own machine and tricks you into approving it. Proposed guards: the approval page shows the machine's name and organization and asks you to confirm the code it prints; codes expire within minutes and work once; only you can approve; attempts are rate-limited.

    • Fleet central takes each machine's identity, organization, enrollment state and expiry only from the new login;
    • your admin access works through the sign-in service;
    • an old enrollment credential, or a missing or invalid new token, is refused even with a valid Access token;
    • a running machine, including a VM, can renew its token, and a revoked machine cannot.
    After that, Access is only an outer gate.

Where work runs: rooms

Everything above says who belongs where. A room says where an agent physically runs, and so which files it can reach. Rooms are not membership: being in a room grants no workspace, and being a member puts you in no room.

RoomExampleWhat it keeps apart
Open roomLaptop Router running directly on your MacLittle. Agents running as your user on that Mac can generally read the same files
Walled-off cornerA sandboxed agent process on that Mac with fenced readsBest effort, for that agent only, as far as its reads are fenced
Locked roomHome VM Router inside a VMThe guest's files, as long as you trust the host and share no folders into it. It is the only real file wall

No organization room rule (your call, 26 October): a rule nothing checks adds nothing. Keeping organizations on separate machines or VMs is your practice; the system declares, checks and shows nothing about it (identity U38).

The agreed first identity round will have each session record its room kind when it starts, and will show which workspaces share one Router. This is designed, not built. Even then nothing proves the record and nothing is refused because of it (your "record and show now, enforce later"). Enrollment proves which Router installation is connecting. It does not prove which machine or VM that Router runs on, and no identity is derived from hardware.

Where this differs from the identity design, and its sources
  • People may be collaborators; the identity design had only bot collaborators (its E9).
  • The identity design still mentions a machine serving two organizations (its V9); your rule says one Router, one organization.
  • The Fleet framework's old note "machines map to one workspace" (Q-B11) is replaced: a Fleet machine may serve several workspaces of its organization (identity U6).
  • Machine login changes. The identity design says a machine proves itself only through its existing enrollment (U17), with no platform-signed machine identity yet (requirements, line 110) and platform identity later (alternatives). Your decision replaces that with issuer tokens for every machine, device code for machines you set up, and the three-step switch. The identity design's rule of no new private key on each machine still holds: a VM creator's signing key lives with the creator, not in each VM. The identity design needs revising before planning.
